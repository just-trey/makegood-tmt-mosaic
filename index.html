The five-minute version

Pick a part shape, drop in an SVG or a photo, and each flat color becomes its own recessed region. Export a 3MF and it's pre-mapped to AMS filament slots — open it in Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer, or Snapmaker Orca and print.

Your session autosaves as you go. Come back after closing the tab or a reload and you'll be offered to restore it — nothing applies or rebuilds until you say so.

Everything below matches a section in the left panel, in the same order.

Left panel · Part

Choosing a part

The dropdown lists real MakeGood TMT parts — pick one (Wheel, Footrest, …) and its actual mesh loads, with pockets cut directly into it. Disc (reference) is a plain flat-plate insert sized by hand, kept as a quick reference shape.

Body / blank color is the fallback color of the physical blank (neutral grey or a plain filament) — it's what the body prints when no artwork color is grouped into the base. To print an artwork color as the body instead, group it into the Base row in Colors detected below (see "→ base" there) — an artwork base always wins over this fallback.

Download design template gives you that part's design face as a true-to-size (1:1 mm) SVG — its real printable outline, with any no-print gaps already punched out. Draw over it in your editor and load the result under Artwork. Where a surface covers more than one printed piece, dashed lines mark the joins and the labels name the piece each area lands on — anything you draw across a dashed line gets cut into both pieces, and lines up as well as your print and assembly do.

A part with hardware variants (Standard vs Kit) shows a version picker above the part list — switching reloads only the pieces that actually differ between versions, confirming first if you've already customized one of them.

Left panel · Artwork

Preparing your artwork

You can load either an SVG or a PNG/JPG image. The format is detected from the file itself, so the extension doesn't have to be right.

For an SVG, use flat, distinct fill colors — no gradients or pattern fills, those get flagged and skipped. Each unique fill becomes a candidate color region before you even touch the fit controls.

An image has no flat fills to read, so it gets quantized into them: the app picks a palette from the image, snaps every pixel to it, and traces the result back to outlines. Photos work — smoothing and speckle removal are tuned automatically from how detailed the image is, so a photo gets a heavier hand than a logo without you telling it which is which. A transparent background stays transparent and cuts nothing.

A loaded image gets two sliders on its row. Colors is how many flat colors to reduce to — the readout under it tells you how many it actually found, which can be fewer (a three-color logo stays three however high you push it) and how many separate regions that came to. Detail trades fineness against printability: lower merges small specks away and smooths outlines, higher keeps them. Both re-trace the image when you let go of the slider, not while you drag.

Colors and Auto-merge below do different jobs and don't fight: Colors decides which regions exist, Auto-merge decides which of them share a filament slot. An image's palette comes out already separated past the "Slight" setting, so that default has nothing left to do on one.

For an assembly part, design over the template you downloaded in the Part section above so the size is right. If an editor drops the document's millimeter size on export (Affinity writes width="100%"), the app scales the document's viewBox to fit the part face — setting the editor document to millimeters keeps it exact.

The thumbnail strip below the dropzone is a small built-in library of tileable patterns (Cow, Dalmatian, Zebra, Tiger) — click one to load it like an uploaded SVG. On an assembly part it defaults to Fill mode, since a pattern exists to repeat across a surface rather than sit as a single sticker.

Each design you load appears as a named row below the dropzone — click × to remove it. Click a row to make it the active design; the fit sliders and the on-face gizmo below always edit whichever row is active.

A design loaded onto a surface that already has one starts stepped slightly across and down from it, so it isn't hidden underneath. They still overlap, and a warning names both until you move, rescale or separate them — two recesses cut into the same spot export as two inlays claiming the same space, which a slicer resolves however it likes. It won't stop you exporting. Two designs both set to Fill always warn, and there moving them can't help: switch one to Sticker or put it on another surface. The check compares each design's bounding box, so something nested neatly inside another — a logo inside a frame — can trip it even though the recesses never meet.

On a part with more than one design surface (like the chair body), each row gets a zone dropdown to pick which surface that design lands on. A new design starts on the first zone; switch it with the dropdown, use "+zone" to place the same design a second time on another surface, or pick "All zones" to stamp it onto every surface at once (slow on the chair — it recuts all five).

On an assembly part, each row also switches between Sticker and Fill. Sticker places one copy of the design. Fill repeats it across the whole surface — one repeat per SVG document, so a tileable design whose edges line up comes out seamless. Scale sets how big each repeat is and Offset shifts the whole pattern; at very small scales the fill is refused (with a warning) rather than run to a halt. Colors still pool across every design in the scene, so a black pattern shares its filament slot with black artwork elsewhere.

Left panel · Artwork fit

Positioning artwork on the part

Margin sets the auto-fit border in flat-plate mode (hidden in assembly mode, where the design maps 1:1 onto the part face). Scale multiplies on top of that — over 100% will bleed past the part's edge. Rotation spins the artwork about its own center. Flip H mirrors left-to-right, useful when text reads backwards after import.

You can also grab the artwork directly in the 3D view: drag inside the selection frame to move it, drag a corner handle to scale, or drag the green handle to rotate — the sliders track the drag and vice versa. The frame follows the surface, so on a curved part it curves too; if it turns amber, the design's center has moved off the design surface and part of the artwork won't be cut. On a part with more than one design surface, clicking a surface (away from the selection frame) binds the active design to it — the same thing the zone dropdown in the Artwork list does.

Left panel · Depth

How deep recesses cut

Depth is measured down from the top face. Set a default for all colors, then override individual ones in Colors detected below for fine detail work. Check "Recess bg too" to also cut the background instead of leaving it flush. A color with its own depth is highlighted in the list below and has a "↺" button to put it back under this default — if changing this field seems to do nothing, those rows are why. On a flat shape, a depth the plate can't hold is cut at the nearest depth it can — a warning says which color and what was actually cut, so raise Thickness in Part if you want it deeper. A depth of 0 cuts nothing at all, so it's raised to 0.2mm; a depth below 0.2mm is cut exactly as asked, with a note that it needs a layer height finer than the usual 0.2mm to show up. To stop cutting a color entirely, use "→ base" on its row to print it in the body instead.

Left panel · Colors detected

Merging into AMS slots

The Auto-merge slider collapses visually similar colors automatically — None turns it off, Slight (the default) dedupes near-identical export artifacts, Medium and Strong collapse shading ramps toward fewer filaments. It's fully reversible: drag it back down and colors pull back apart. Click a merged color's "×" to pull just that one out without affecting the rest of the group.

You can also merge manually: drag one color onto another (the ⠿ grip marks what's draggable) to combine them into a single AMS slot sharing one depth — or, if dragging isn't convenient, pick the other color from that row's own "Merge with…" dropdown instead; both do the same thing. A merged slot prints in its dominant member's real color, not a blended average. To split a group back up, click its members' "×" swatches (each pulled color stays out until you re-merge it).

The Base row pinned at the top is a slot too — one that prints as the body instead of being cut as a recess. "→ base" on a color (or a whole merged group) makes it the base, swapping out whatever was there; to combine several colors into the base instead, drag them onto the Base row. It shows every color you've grouped in, and it always prints its dominant member's color. Click a base color's "×" to send it back to being cut. Empty the base entirely and the body falls back to the plain Body / blank color picker up in Part.

The line below the color list ("N colors → M AMS slots needed") always includes the body's own slot in the count — that's the "+1" you'll see even before anything is merged. Every AMS/toolchanger unit holds 4, so past 4 the line stands out and a pill appears saying you'll need a second unit or manual filament swaps; the Bambus daisy-chain up to 16 (25 on an H2D across both nozzles), while the Snapmaker U1's 4 toolheads are a hard ceiling. Only going past the printer's real maximum turns the line red, and even then exporting still works. Both update the moment you switch printers below in Export, so you can compare before committing to a file. Dismissing the pill keeps it gone until the count or the printer actually changes.

Left panel · Export

Getting a printable file

Pick your printer, then export. The 3MF is print-ready for Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer, or Snapmaker Orca with colors pre-assigned to filament slots. The STL set is a per-color .zip fallback for slicers that don't read pre-mapped 3MF color data.

About

The TMT

TMT Mosaic prepares color-inlay artwork for the Toddler Mobility Trainer (TMT) — a free, open-source 3D-printable mobility device for children ages 1–8. Printable part files and assembly instructions live at 3d-mobility.org.
