The five-minute version

Pick a part, then drop in an SVG or a photo. Each flat color becomes its own recessed region. Export a 3MF and it arrives pre-mapped to filament slots. Open it in Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer, or Snapmaker Orca and print.

Your session autosaves as you go. Reload or close the tab, and you'll be offered a restore when you come back. Nothing applies or rebuilds until you say so.

Everything below matches a section in the left panel, in the same order.

Left panel · Part

Choosing a part

The dropdown lists real MakeGood TMT parts: Wheel, Hubcap and Footrest. Pick one and the actual part loads, with pockets cut directly into it.

Body / blank color is the fallback color of the physical part (neutral grey or a plain filament). The body prints in it when no artwork color is grouped into the base. To print an artwork color as the body instead, group it into the Base row in Colors detected. An artwork base always wins over this fallback.

Download design template gives you that part's design face as a true-to-size (1:1 mm) SVG. It is the real printable outline, with any no-print gaps already punched out. Draw over it in your editor, then load the result under Artwork. Where a design face covers more than one printed piece, dashed lines mark the joins. Labels name the piece each area lands on. Anything you draw across a dashed line is cut into both pieces. It lines up as well as your print and assembly do.

Hubcap diameter appears for the Hubcap. It is built to the size you ask for, instead of being a fixed mesh. The four mounting clips stay put. The disc around them grows or shrinks, keeping its 1 mm angled edge. The number is capped at your printer's bed, so switching to a smaller machine may bring it down. It won't go below the size that still covers the clips. Re-download the design template after changing it: the template is drawn to whatever size is set.

Its plate has been checked by hand up to 220 mm, on 256 mm and 270 mm beds. At those sizes the export puts the part and the prime tower where a real print proved they both fit. Go bigger, or use another bed, and nothing has been verified. It exports centred, and the prime tower goes in the freest corner. Where every corner overlaps a part the app says so, and where that is true of every plate printing a tower it saves no position at all and your slicer places it. Check both in your slicer before printing.

Cut to artwork shape replaces the round disc with the outline of the artwork loaded on it. The picture and the part become the same shape. A character or a logo prints as its own silhouette instead of sitting inside a circle. It needs exactly one piece of artwork loaded (remove extras first), with a real transparent background. That transparency is what defines the cutline, so a fully opaque image has no edge to cut to. Only Sticker placement is offered while this is on: Fill has nothing to repeat across a shape that keeps changing. Turn it off, or remove the artwork, and the part reverts to a plain circle.

While it's on, the Fit controls reshape the part as well as the picture. Scale, Rotation, the flips and Offset move the two together, because they are the same object. Re-download the design template after any of them: it is drawn to the silhouette, not to a disc. Nothing may overhang the 280 mm wheel. A shape that would is scaled down until it fits, artwork included. The app says so rather than leaving the size control looking broken.

While it's on, any color that reaches the outline is cut the disc's full 3 mm instead of being recessed. The rim of your shape then prints in that color. Without it you'd see a band of base color from every angle but straight on. Colors in the middle of the part still recess at their normal depth. The app lists which colors this applied to. It overrides a depth you set by hand, at the edge only. That color's interior regions still cut to your number. This is the shape's outside edge. If your silhouette encloses a hole, the rim around the hole still prints in the base color.

Left panel · Artwork

Preparing your artwork

SVG, PNG/JPG, WebP, GIF and BMP all load directly. The format is detected from the file itself, so the extension doesn't have to be right. TIFF is the one thing no browser can open. Re-export it as a PNG.

For an SVG, use flat, distinct fill colors. Gradients and pattern fills are flagged and skipped. Each unique fill becomes a candidate color region before you touch the fit controls.

Photos work. An image has no flat colors to read, so the app reduces it to some and traces the edges. Smoothing is set from the image's own detail, so a photo gets a heavier hand than a logo. A transparent background cuts nothing.

A loaded image gets two sliders on its row. Colors is how many flat colors to reduce to. The readout under it tells you how many it actually found, and how many separate regions those came to. It can find fewer: a three-color logo stays three however high you push the slider. Detail trades fineness against printability. Lower merges small specks away and smooths outlines; higher keeps them, and on a part, down to what your nozzle can print at the size the design sits at. Both sliders re-trace the image when you let go, not while you drag. That is also when the placed size is read, so after resizing the part or the design a long way, nudge Colors or Detail to trace it again at the new size.

Colors and Auto-merge do different jobs and don't fight. Colors decides which regions exist. Auto-merge decides which of them share a filament slot. An image's colors come out far enough apart that the "Slight" default has nothing left to do on one.

For an assembly part, design over the template you downloaded in the Part section, so the size is right. Set your editor document to millimeters. Some editors drop the mm size on export (Affinity writes width="100%"). The app then scales the drawing to fit the part face.

Each design you load appears as a named row below the dropzone. Click × to remove it. Click a row to make it the active design. The fit sliders and the on-face gizmo always edit whichever row is active.

A design loaded onto a design face that already has one starts stepped across and down, so it isn't hidden underneath. The step grows with the designs there, so small ones often land clear. If they still overlap, a warning names both until you move, rescale or separate them. Two recesses cut into the same spot export as two inlays claiming the same space. A slicer resolves that however it likes, and won't stop you exporting. Two designs both set to Fill always warn, and moving them can't help. Switch one to Sticker. It also checks how much artwork reaches the other, so a logo inside a frame no longer trips it.

On an assembly part, each row also switches between Sticker and Fill. Sticker places the design once. Fill repeats it across the whole part as tiles, one tile per SVG document. A tileable design whose edges line up comes out seamless. Scale sets how big each tile is, and Offset shifts the whole pattern. When a fill can't be tiled, the app says why and leaves you one tile rather than running to a halt. Colors still pool across every design in the scene. A black pattern shares its filament slot with black artwork elsewhere.

Left panel · Artwork fit

Positioning artwork on the part

The design maps 1:1 onto the part face. Scale multiplies on top of that; over 100% bleeds past the part's edge. Rotation spins the artwork about its own center. Flip H mirrors left to right, which helps when text reads backwards after import. Flip V mirrors top to bottom. Both are mirrors, not rotations. To turn artwork the right way up, use Rotation.

You can also grab the artwork directly in the 3D view. Drag inside the selection frame to move it, drag a corner handle to scale, or drag the green handle to rotate. The sliders track the drag, and the drag tracks the sliders. The frame follows the design face, so on a curved part it curves too. If it turns amber, the design's center has moved off that face. Part of the artwork won't be cut.

Left panel · Depth

How deep recesses cut

Depth is measured down from the top face. Set a default for all colors, then override individual ones in Colors detected for fine detail work. Check "Recess bg too" to cut the background as well, instead of leaving it flush. It then prints as its own color and takes a filament slot. A color with its own depth is highlighted in the list, and the Depth panel says how many are doing it, with a "Reset all" to put them back. Its "↺" button does the same for one row. A depth of 0 cuts nothing at all, so it is raised to 0.2mm. A depth below 0.2mm is cut exactly as asked. A note says it needs a layer height finer than the usual 0.2mm. A depth deeper than the part has material for is cut at the deepest it can take, and says so. That is the whole part, not its wall, so a pocket deeper than a thin wall can still break through without a warning: check the cut in the 3D view before printing. To stop cutting a color entirely, use "→ base" on its row to print it in the body instead.

Left panel · Colors detected

Merging into filament slots

The Auto-merge slider groups colors that look alike. None turns it off. Slight (the default) catches near-identical copies of one color. Medium and Strong pull gradients down to fewer filaments. It is fully reversible: drag the slider back down and colors pull apart again. Click a merged color's "×" to pull just that one out, leaving the rest of the group alone.

You can also merge by hand. Drag one color onto another to combine them into a single filament slot, sharing one depth. The ⠿ grip marks what's draggable. If dragging isn't convenient, pick the other color from that row's "Merge with…" dropdown instead. Both do the same thing. A merged slot prints in the group's main color, not a blended average. To split a group back up, click its members' "×" swatches. Each pulled color stays out until you re-merge it.

The Base row pinned at the top is a slot too. It prints as the body instead of being cut as a recess. "→ base" on a color, or on a whole merged group, adds it to the base. Dragging one onto the Base row does the same thing. The row shows every color you've grouped in, and always prints the group's main color. Click a base color's "×" to send it back to being cut. Empty the base and the body falls back to the Body / blank color picker in Part.

The line below the color list ("N colors → M slots needed") always counts the body's own slot. That is the "+1" you see even before anything is merged. Every AMS or toolchanger unit holds 4. Past 4, the line stands out and a pill appears naming the limit and offering to merge two colors. Merging is not the only way out: you can print a color in the body with "→ base", add a second unit where the printer takes one, or swap filament by hand at a pause. The pill names one because a warning that lists every option is harder to act on, not because the others stopped working. The Bambus daisy-chain up to 16, or 25 on an H2D across both nozzles. The Snapmaker U1's 4 toolheads are a hard ceiling. Only going past the printer's real maximum turns the line red, and even then exporting still works. Both update the moment you switch printers in Export, so you can compare before committing to a file. Dismissing the pill keeps it gone until the count or the printer changes.

Left panel · Export

Getting a printable file

Pick your printer, then export. The 3MF is print-ready for Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer, or Snapmaker Orca with colors pre-assigned to filament slots.

A long rebuild shows a curtain with a Cancel button, and most of the wait on a detailed design stops the moment you press it. Once the cutting itself has started it finishes the part it is on first, so the button reads "Cancelling…" for a moment. The parts go back to showing uncut, and Export switches off until you build again. Your settings are untouched, and still saved, which reloading the page never was.

The box above the button says what you are about to get: how many parts, how many plates, and a swatch per filament, with the parts listed plate by plate. It follows the design on screen, so a color change or a merge updates it before you commit, and you see the size of the print before the download starts rather than after. Parts whose plates are not pinned in advance, a hubcap cut to artwork shape for instance, are arranged as the file is written, so those show parts and filaments without a plate count.

About

The TMT

TMT Mosaic prepares color-inlay artwork for the Toddler Mobility Trainer (TMT). The TMT is a free, open-source 3D-printable mobility device for children ages 1–8. Printable part files and assembly instructions live at 3d-mobility.org.
